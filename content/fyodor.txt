A man is born without his consent, given a consciousness he never requested, thrown into a world whose rules he did not write, and then expected to make something meaningful of the entire affair.
He spends his youth preparing to live. His adulthood struggling to live. His old age remembering when he might have lived.
And then he dies.
The universe offers no explanation.
But here is where Dostoevsky becomes more disturbing than Camus.
What if man does not actually want happiness?
What if suffering is not merely an unfortunate interruption of life, but something to which man becomes secretly attached?
Consider the man who knows exactly what is destroying him, understands every consequence, possesses the intelligence to escape, and yet continues.
He is not ignorant.
He is not necessarily helpless.
He is conscious.
And perhaps that is his greatest punishment.
In Notes from Underground, Dostoevsky explores the terrifying possibility that human beings will sometimes act against their own interests simply to prove that they are not machines governed by reason.

### The Cruelty of Karma

An exploration in the spirit of Fyodor Dostoevsky

There is a peculiar comfort in believing that the universe keeps accounts.

That every cruelty shall be repaid. Every kindness rewarded. Every betrayal answered. Every tear entered into some invisible ledger, awaiting the day when justice finally arrives.

We call this karma.

And perhaps the most disturbing thing about karma is not whether it exists.

It is why man so desperately needs it to exist.

For what would become of him if he discovered that the wicked sometimes prosper, the innocent sometimes perish, and the universe has no obligation to correct either outcome?

Would he still be good?

Would he still forgive?

Would he still rise from his bed each morning and participate in this dreadful comedy called civilization?

Or would he finally admit that much of what he called morality was merely a transaction with eternity?

### I. The Merchant of Good Deeds

Imagine a man who spends his entire life being good.

He feeds the hungry. He comforts the miserable. He refuses to deceive, even when deception would benefit him.

But somewhere, buried beneath his noble conduct, lives a small and rather embarrassing expectation.

Surely, someday, life will reward me.

And so he waits.

Years pass.

His dishonest neighbour grows wealthy. His cruel employer receives admiration. Those who betrayed him sleep peacefully, while he lies awake rehearsing conversations that ended years ago.

At first, he is patient.

Then confused.

Then bitter.

Finally, one evening, he raises his eyes toward heaven and demands:

Where is my reward?

But observe the tragedy.

Was he good because goodness was worth pursuing?

Or had he merely entered into a contract with God, expecting payment at a later date?

What a dreadful discovery it would be for a virtuous man to realize that he had not been virtuous at all, but merely a patient investor in divine justice.

And yet, who among us is entirely innocent of this?

Even the man who proudly announces that he expects nothing from anybody may secretly expect to be admired for expecting nothing.

Human vanity is extraordinarily resourceful.

It can turn even humility into a form of pride.

### II. The Innocent Child and the Debt of a Previous Life

Here we encounter a far darker question.

Suppose a child is born into suffering.

Before the child has learned language, before it has understood right from wrong, before it has committed a single conscious cruelty, life has already begun its punishment.

A believer in certain forms of karmic justice might answer:

Perhaps the child carries the consequences of a previous existence.

But what have we accomplished by saying this?

We have explained the suffering, perhaps, but have we justified it?

This is where Dostoevsky's Ivan Karamazov would become intolerably restless.

Ivan's rebellion was not simply against the existence of God. It was against the possibility of a cosmic harmony purchased at the expense of innocent suffering.

Imagine telling a suffering child that its pain belongs to an immense moral arrangement it cannot remember, comprehend, or contest.

Would such an explanation comfort the child?

Or would it comfort the adults who cannot bear the thought that innocence offers no protection against cruelty?

There is an important distinction here. In Indian philosophical traditions, karma is not always understood as punishment administered by a cosmic judge. Different schools understand it through causation, intention, rebirth, and liberation. Nor does every interpretation claim that all present suffering is deserved.

Nevertheless, a disturbing question survives even the most sophisticated interpretation:

At what point does our desire to explain suffering become an excuse for accepting it?

Perhaps the greater moral achievement is not discovering why the child suffers.

Perhaps it is refusing to allow the child to suffer alone.

And if some heavenly accountant objects that we have interfered with the child's deserved consequences, then perhaps heaven itself must learn compassion.

### III. The Wicked Man Who Sleeps Peacefully

There is something almost offensive about the happiness of a wicked man.

Not his wickedness alone.

His happiness.

He has deceived, exploited, humiliated, and abandoned others. He has left behind lives that must now be painfully reconstructed.

And yet he eats well.

He laughs loudly.

He marries, travels, accumulates possessions, and grows old beneath a roof purchased, perhaps, through the suffering of others.

Meanwhile, the person he injured remains awake at three in the morning, unable to understand why memory has become a prison.

People offer reassurance:

Karma will get him.

But suppose it does not.

Suppose he dies peacefully, surrounded by loving grandchildren, without ever experiencing the slightest remorse.

What then?

Will we invent another lifetime to complete the punishment?

Will we postpone justice indefinitely rather than confront the possibility that justice is not guaranteed?

Here Dostoevsky would force us to examine a particularly humiliating aspect of our nature.

We do not merely want the wicked man to become good.

We want him to suffer.

We want him to understand, through pain, what he made others endure.

And sometimes, if he sincerely repented, apologized, and became a compassionate human being, we would find ourselves strangely dissatisfied.

For repentance would transform him.

But punishment would satisfy us.

How easily our hunger for justice becomes indistinguishable from our appetite for revenge.

We call upon karma not always because we love goodness, but sometimes because we cannot tolerate the thought of an enemy escaping our resentment.

And resentment, when preserved long enough, becomes a peculiar form of intimacy.

The man who injured us may have forgotten our existence.

Yet we continue living with him.

We invite him into every quiet evening.

We allow him to sit beside us at dinner.

We carry him into our dreams.

What a terrible victory we grant him.

### IV. The Man Who Punishes Himself

But there is another kind of karma.

One that requires neither reincarnation nor divine intervention.

A man commits a wrong.

Nobody discovers it.

No police officer comes knocking. No witness appears. His reputation remains intact. His bank account remains undisturbed.

The world continues.

But something within him has changed.

He begins constructing arguments.

I had no choice.

Anybody in my position would have done the same.

The other person deserved it.

At first these explanations satisfy him.

Then he must repeat them.

Then he must defend them.

Eventually, his entire personality becomes a fortress erected around one event he cannot bear to examine.

He grows suspicious of honest people because their honesty resembles an accusation.

He mocks compassion because compassion reminds him of what he refused to show.

He despises judgment because he has never stopped judging himself.

And so his punishment begins.

Not with thunder from heaven.

Not with the loss of his fortune.

But with the exhausting necessity of continually proving to himself that he is innocent.

This is the psychological territory of Crime and Punishment.

Raskolnikov's torment is not reducible to fear of legal consequences. His crime becomes entangled with his pride, his theory of exceptional individuals, and his inability to live peacefully with what he has done.

Yet we must resist turning this into another comforting law.

Not every wrongdoer experiences remorse.

Some sleep soundly.

Some possess consciences remarkably skilled at remaining silent.

Guilt is a possible punishment, not a universal guarantee of justice.

And perhaps that is precisely why human beings created courts, laws, and moral obligations.

Because conscience alone cannot be trusted to punish every crime.

### V. The Terrible Arrogance of Saying "Everything Happens for a Reason"

Few sentences are spoken with greater confidence and less knowledge.

A man loses his livelihood.

Everything happens for a reason.

A woman buries someone she loves.

Everything happens for a reason.

An innocent person is betrayed.

It was meant to teach you something.

But who appointed us interpreters of another person's suffering?

Why must every wound become a lesson?

Why must every catastrophe conceal a blessing?

Perhaps a man suffered because another man was cruel.

Perhaps a woman lost something precious because circumstances were merciless.

Perhaps no profound transformation awaits them.

Perhaps there is no reward concealed inside the tragedy.

And perhaps insisting otherwise is simply our inability to endure a world in which terrible things sometimes happen without a redeeming purpose.

There is a difference between discovering meaning after suffering and claiming that suffering was inflicted for the purpose of producing meaning.

The first may be an act of human courage.

The second can become an act of extraordinary cruelty.

For if every tragedy is necessary, then what becomes of our obligation to prevent tragedy?

If every victim is learning a lesson, what becomes of our responsibility to protect the vulnerable?

One might even ask whether the phrase "everything happens for a reason" is sometimes less an expression of faith than a refusal to confront helplessness.

We would rather imagine a cruel plan than admit that there may have been no plan at all.

At least a plan gives suffering an author.

And an author can be questioned.

### VI. The Prisoner Who Falls in Love With His Chains

Now let us consider a man who has suffered for many years.

At first, he longs to escape.

He imagines a different life. He dreams of freedom, prosperity, companionship, and peace.

But slowly something changes.

His suffering becomes familiar.

He knows its rhythms.

He knows how disappointment feels before it arrives. He knows how to survive humiliation. He knows the language of rejection so intimately that kindness begins to sound suspicious.

Then one day, an opportunity appears.

A genuine opportunity.

He could leave.

He could begin again.

And suddenly he is terrified.

For suffering has become more than an experience.

It has become his identity.

Without it, who is he?

If he is no longer the betrayed man, the unfortunate man, the misunderstood man, the man against whom the world has conspired, what remains?

He must now accept responsibility for a future that cannot be explained entirely by his past.

And responsibility can be more frightening than misery.

This is not to say that people choose their traumas or can simply decide to escape hardship. Many prisons are material, economic, and imposed by others.

But sometimes the prison survives even after its door has opened.

The underground man does not merely suffer.

He studies his suffering, polishes it, and sometimes uses it to establish his superiority over those who appear content.

He tells himself that happy people are shallow.

That peaceful people are ignorant.

That only he, in his misery, has understood the terrible machinery of existence.

And in this way, suffering becomes vanity.

A man may become so proud of having survived hell that he no longer knows how to live outside it.

### VII. The Final Insult: What If Karma Does Exist?

Now suppose, after all our objections, karma is real.

Suppose every intention matters.

Every action leaves a consequence.

Every cruelty alters the moral structure of existence, whether or not its consequences become visible within a single lifetime.

Would that solve our problem?

Not entirely.

For then we must confront an even more disturbing possibility.

The person who injured us may be acting from suffering we cannot see.

The cruel father may have been a frightened child.

The dishonest man may have grown up in a world where honesty was punished.

The person who humiliated us may himself have spent decades being humiliated.

This does not excuse their actions.

But it complicates our desire for simple moral arithmetic.

If every person is both the consequence of previous causes and the cause of future consequences, where exactly does punishment end?

At what point do we stop tracing the chain backward and declare:

Here. This person alone is guilty.

And if we ourselves were placed inside the exact circumstances, fears, desires, and limitations of the person we condemn, can we be certain that we would have behaved differently?

We would like to think so.

Perhaps we would.

But our certainty is suspiciously convenient.

The terrible difficulty of compassion is that it asks us to understand people without necessarily absolving them.

And the terrible difficulty of justice is that it asks us to hold people accountable without surrendering to hatred.

Neither is easy.

Neither can be reduced to a proverb about karma.

### VIII. The Man Who No Longer Waits for Justice

Imagine, finally, a man who has abandoned his demand that existence must repay him.

He does not believe that kindness guarantees prosperity.

He does not believe that cruelty always invites punishment.

He does not believe that his suffering makes him superior to anyone else.

He has seen too much to believe in such convenient arrangements.

And yet, strangely, he continues to be kind.

Not because heaven is watching.

Not because some invisible force will reward him.

Not because he expects the people he helps to remember his name.

He is kind because he has witnessed cruelty and refuses to become another instrument of it.

He understands that the world may never repay him.

And for the first time, he is not bargaining with it.

There is something almost unbearable about such a man.

He cannot be purchased with promises of paradise.

He cannot be frightened into goodness by threats of cosmic punishment.

He has chosen his conduct without demanding guarantees.

Perhaps this is not the death of karma.

Perhaps it is the death of our childish understanding of it.

For the deepest moral question is not whether the universe will eventually punish the person who wronged us.

It is whether we can prevent that wrong from determining the person we become.

And yet even this sounds too neat, too comforting, too much like a conclusion written by a man who has never truly suffered.

Dostoevsky would not allow us to leave so easily.

He would return us to the lonely room, the unpaid debts, the unanswered prayers, the memories of people who never apologized, and the unbearable knowledge that our lives might have been different had somebody shown us a little mercy.

Then he would place before us the most difficult question of all.

Not whether we believe in karma.

Not whether God is just.

Not whether the wicked will be punished.

But whether we can continue to love a world that has given us every reason to resent it.

And perhaps, after a long silence, the underground man would whisper:

I do not want the universe to explain my suffering.

I want it to admit that I suffered.

I want someone, somewhere, to say that what happened mattered.

And if no one says it?

Then I fear I shall spend my entire life demanding justice from a silence that does not even know my name.

The cruelest possibility is not that karma fails to punish those who have wronged us. It is that we may spend our only life waiting to witness their punishment, and mistake that waiting for living.
